Market Basket Analysis (MBA)

 Sales analysis:
    What items occur together in the ´basket´?

 Results:

  · expressed in the form of rules
  · can be immediately applied

 Application:

  · Planning and layout of shops
  · offer of coupons, limited discounts
  · ´packaging´ of products

I. MRÁZOVÁ: DOBÝVÁNÍ ZNALOSTÍ                   4
Association Rules

How are the respective products related?

Association rules should be:

 · easy-to-understand: after there is a relationship found, it can be
    easily verified

 · applicable: they comprise useful information, that can lead to
    further interventions

Association rules should be NOT:

 · trivial: everyone already knows the results
 · inexplicable: there does not exist an explanation for them, and

    they do not lead to an action

MBA for shop comparison

Virtual items:

· Indicate the type of the shop, where the transaction took
    place

· Do not correspond to any product or service

A comparison of new and already existing shops:

1. Provide transaction data from new shops for a certain time
2. Provide about the same amount of data from existing shops
3. Use MBA to find association rules in both sets of transactions
4. In particular, consider association rules with virtual items

MBA ­ How to do that?

Item - product or service offer

Transaction contains one or more items

Frequency table

· indicates the number of co-occurrences of any two
   items within the performed transactions (i.e., how
   many times were these two products purchased
   together)

· the values on its diagonal correspond to the number
   of transactions that contain the respective item

The form of the rules:

          IF Condition THEN Conclusion

               ( ~ Rule_r : IF Item_i THEN Item_j . )

Questions:

  How good are the found association rules?

   · support
   · confidence
   · lift

  How to find the association rules automatically?

I. MRÁZOVÁ: DOBÝVÁNÍ ZNALOSTÍ                          9
Support and confidence

Support: How often can we use the rule?

                          Nr_of_transactions_that_contain_i_and_j  · 100 %
Support(Rule_r) =

                                    Nr_of_all_transactions

Confidence: How reliable are the results of the rules?

Confidence(Rule_r) =  Nr_of_transactions_that_contain_i_and_j

                      Nr_of_transactions_that_contain_i            · 100 %

                      I. MRÁZOVÁ: DOBÝVÁNÍ ZNALOSTÍ                         10
Support and confidence - example

Rule 1: If the customer buys bread
             then he buys also butter.

Rule 2: If the customer buys coffee
             then he buys also butter.

Support ( Rule_1 ) = 3 / 5 · 100 % = 60 %
Support ( Rule_2 ) = 2 / 5 · 100 % = 40 %
Confidence ( Rule_1 ) = 3 / 4 · 100 % = 75 %
Confidence ( Rule_2 ) = 2 / 3 · 100 % = 66 %

I. MRÁZOVÁ: DOBÝVÁNÍ ZNALOSTÍ                 11
The lift of a rule

Lift: How many times is it better to use the rule for
      prediction instead of just assuming its result?

                                         p(i_a_j)
                  Lift (Rule_r) =

                                        p(i) · p(j)

If it holds that Lift < 1:

 the rule is worse than a random choice for prediction
 NEGATION of the result can yield a better rule

    IF Condition THEN NOT Conclusion.

I. MRÁZOVÁ: DOBÝVÁNÍ ZNALOSTÍ  12
The lift of a rule ­ an example

Rule: If the customer buys milk
            then he buys also butter.

Support ( Rule ) = 1 / 5 · 100 % = 20 %
Confidence ( Rule ) = 1 / 1 · 100 % = 100 %
Lift ( Rule ) = ( 1 / 5 ) / ( ( 1 / 5 ) · ( 4 / 5 ) ) = 5 / 4 = 1.25

The main steps of MBA

 Choose appropriate items at an adequate level

 Generate rules based on the frequency table

   · compute (conditional) probabilities of item occurrence
      and occurrence of their combinations in the transactions

   · Restrict the search by a threshold value put on support

 Find the best rules through analyzing the probabilities

   · Overcome the limits given by the number of items and
      their combinations in ´interesting´ transactions

I. MRÁZOVÁ: DOBÝVÁNÍ ZNALOSTÍ  14
MBA ­ the choice of appropriate items

Obtaining the transaction data:

  Often a worse quality, requires a more extensive pre-
    processing

  The relevance of the items can change with time
  An adequate level of processing:

     · A growing number of combinations of the respective items
     · The results can be immediately applied (specific items)
     · Rules with a sufficiently high support (frequent occurrence

         in the data set)

I. MRÁZOVÁ: DOBÝVÁNÍ ZNALOSTÍ  15
Taxonomy:

the hierarchy of the categories

MBA ­ the complexity of generated rules:

 · In the beginning, use more general items
 · Later, generate rules for specific items based only on the

    transactions that contain these items

MBA ­ Applicable results:

The considered items should occur in roughly the same number
of transactions:
 · Move rare items to higher levels in the taxonomy (where

    they appear relatively more frequently)
 · Leave the more common items at lower levels (in order to

    not allow the most frequent items to dominate the rules)

I. MRÁZOVÁ: DOBÝVÁNÍ ZNALOSTÍ    16
Virtual items:

exceed the framework of a traditional taxonomy

  Blur the boundary between certain types of products in
    original items

     · E.g., (company) brands - Calvin Klein

  Can contain information about the transaction itself

     · anonymous (day of week, time, etc.)
     · signed (information about the customers and their behavior)

  Can impact redundancies in the rules

     · Just one virtual item corresponds to the items from the taxonomy
         (´If product_Skoda then Skoda.´)

     · Both the virtual and general items occur in the rule at the same
         time (´If product_Skoda and small car then tent´ instead of ´If Fabia
         then tent´)

I. MRÁZOVÁ: DOBÝVÁNÍ ZNALOSTÍ  17
MBA ­ generating of the rules

 Compute the frequency table:

   · Provides the information about, what combinations of
       the respective items appear most frequently in the
       transactions

   · Can be used to determine the basic probabilities
       necessary to assess the impact of the generated rules

 Obtain useful rules:

   · The lift should be bigger than 1

         A low lift can be increased by negating the rules
         Negated rules might be less applicable than the original

            ones

   · reduce the number of generated rules - PRUNING

I. MRÁZOVÁ: DOBÝVÁNÍ ZNALOSTÍ                                      18
Pruning according to minimum support

Elimination of less frequent items

  The undertaken actions should concern enough
    transactions

  Two options:

     · Elimination of rare items (and a subsequent
        elimination of corresponding rules)

     · Use a taxonomy to create general items (generalized
        items should meet the set criteria - threshold)

  variable minimum support => a cascade effect

I. MRÁZOVÁ: DOBÝVÁNÍ ZNALOSTÍ  19
MBA ­ Algorithm APRIORI (R. Agrawal)

  Generation of the association rules

       Looking for frequent item-sets

 ~ Frequent item-sets:

     · Combinations (~ conjunctions) of categories, that reach
        a pre-set frequency on the data ~ ´minsup´ support

     · When looking for combinations of the length k, we use
        the known combinations of the length k ­ 1 ~ breadth-
        first generation of combinations

     · To generate the combinations of the length k, we demand
        all their sub-combinations of the length k ­ 1 to fulfill the
        preset frequency requirement

I. MRÁZOVÁ: DOBÝVÁNÍ ZNALOSTÍ  20
MBA ­ Algorithm APRIORI (continued)

 ~ Frequent item-sets (continued):

      · After finding the combinations with a satisfiable frequency, the
         association rules will be created

             ( supercombination_frequency  combination_frequency )

 Each combination Comb will be divided into all possible pairs
    of sub-combinations Ant and Suc such that:
             Suc = Comb ­ Ant

            ( Ant  Suc = {} and Ant  Suc = Comb )

 Then, for the considered rule Ant = > Suc :

      · support corresponds to the frequency of the combination Comb

      · confidence corresponds to the ratio of the frequencies of the
         combinations Comb and Ant

I. MRÁZOVÁ: DOBÝVÁNÍ ZNALOSTÍ  21
MBA ­ Algorithm APRIORI

Step 1: Assign to L1 all the categories that reach at
         least the required frequency

Step 2: Set k = 2

Step 3: Until Lk-1  {}

 Step 3.1: Based on Lk-1 and by means of the function
             APRIORI-GEN, generate a set of candidates Ck

 Step 3.2: Assign to Lk all those combinations from Ck ,
             that reached at least the required frequency

 Step 3.3: Increment k

I. MRÁZOVÁ: DOBÝVÁNÍ ZNALOSTÍ                              22
The function APRIORI-GEN ( Lk-1 )

Step 1: For all pairs of combinations
          Combp , Combq from Lk-1

 Step 1.1: If Combp and Combq match in k - 2
             categories, add Combp  Combq to Ck

Step 2: For each combination Comb from Ck
 Step 2.1: If any of their sub-combinations of the

              length k - 1 is not contained in Lk-1,
              remove Comb from Ck

MBA ­ Dissociation rules

  Rule: IF A AND NOT B THEN C.

     · Introduce new items complementary to the original items
     · If the transaction does not contain the original item, it will

        contain its negation

 Disadvantages:

     · Twice as many items
     · Increased size of transactions
     · Negated items ocur more often than the original ones

        (and the rules with all the items negated are more difficult
        to exploit: ´IF NOT A AND NOT B THEN NOT C.´)

Time-series analysis by means of MBA

 A cause ­ effect analysis:

    · Information about the time, or event sequence to determine when
       the transactions occurred (one in relation to the other)

    · an identification of the customer may be necessary

 A transfer of the problem to MBA:

    · Introduction of new items to transactions that occurred before the
       considered event (for the causes) and after the considered event
       (for the effects); duplicate items are subsequently removed from
       the transactions

    · A window: ´snapshot´ of all the data, that occurred during a certain
       time period (e.g., all transactions from the last month)

         · Trends for rare items

 Provides clear and understandable results

   · IF - THEN - rules that are immediately applicable

 Data mining (without target output values)

   · Important when processing large amounts of data
      without additional a-priori knowledge

 Allows to process data of a variable length

 Computational costs:

      the space of association rules is exponential, O(2m),
            - m is the number of the considered items

      Taxonomies and virtual items are necessary

 Difficulties to determine an adequate number of items:

      the items should have about the same frequency
      rare items shall be considered on higher levels in the taxonomy and shall involve

          variable threshold values for minimum support pruning
            => high minimum support and confidence can be used to prune less useful and
                 too general items

x ... still, MBA tends to produce excessive numbers of rules!

         ­ thousands, tens of thousands, millions, ...

MBA ­ Problems with association mining

 Single minsup: assumes that all items in the
  data are of the same nature and/or have similar
  frequencies.

x Yet in many applications, some items occur
   quite frequently in the data, while others
   appear rather rarely.
   · e.g., in a supermarket, people buy food processor
      and cooking pan much less frequently than they
      buy bread and milk.

I. MRÁZOVÁ: DATA MINING  29
MBA ­ Rare Item Problem

 If the frequencies of items vary a lot, we might
  encounter two problems:

    · If minsup is set too high, those rules that involve rare
       items will not be found.

    · To find rules that involve both frequent and rare items,
       minsup should be set very low.

        => This may cause combinatorial explosion because
             those frequent items will be associated with one
             another in all possible ways.

I. MRÁZOVÁ: DATA MINING                                         30
Multiple minsups model

 The minimum support of a rule is expressed in terms of minimum
   item supports (MIS) of the items that appear in the rule.

 Each item can have a minimum item support.

 By providing different MIS values for different items, the user can
   express different support requirements for different rules.

 To prevent very frequent items and very rare items from appearing
   in the same itemsets, we introduce a support difference constraint:

                       maxis{sup(i)} - minis{sup(i)}  ,
    where 0    1 is the user-specified maximum support difference; it
     is the same for all itemsets.

I. MRÁZOVÁ: DATA MINING  31
Minsup of a rule

 Let MIS(i) be the minimum item support (MIS)
  value of item i. The minsup of a rule R is the
  lowest MIS value of the items in the rule.

 i.e., a rule R: a1, a2, ..., ak  ak+1, ..., ar
  satisfies its minimum support if its actual

  support is  min(MIS(a1), MIS(a2), ..., MIS(ar)).

I. MRÁZOVÁ: DATA MINING  32
Minsup of a rule ­ an example

  Consider the following items:
          bread, shoes, clothes

        · The user-specified MIS values are as follows:
            MIS(bread) = 2% MIS(shoes) = 0.1% MIS(clothes) = 0.2%

        · The following rule does not satisfy its minsup:

            R1: clothes  bread [sup=0.15%,conf =70%]

                 sup (R1) = 0.15% < MIS(clothes) = 0.2%
        · The following rule satisfies its minsup:

            R2: clothes  shoes [sup=0.15%,conf =70%]
                 sup (R2) = 0.15%  MIS(shoes) = 0.1%

I. MRÁZOVÁ: DATA MINING                                            33
Downward closure property

 Downward closure property (~ the key idea for a-priori):
   any subsets of a frequent itemset are also frequent itemsets

x In the new model, this property no longer holds

     · Minimum item supports, namely, allow having higher minimum supports for
         rules that involve only frequent items, and having lower minimum supports
         for rules with less frequent items.

· e.g., consider four items ­ 1, 2, 3 and 4 in a database. Their minimum item
    supports are

MIS(1) = 10%  MIS(2) = 20%
MIS(3) = 5%   MIS(4) = 6%

{1, 2} with support 9% is infrequent, but {1, 2, 3} and {1, 2, 4}
could be frequent => we cannot discard {1, 2}

              I. MRÁZOVÁ: DATA MINING                                          34
How to deal with the problem?

 Sort all items in I according to their MIS values in an
   ascending order; I denotes the set of all items

 The order is then fixed and used for all subsequent
   operations throughout the algorithm in each itemset.

 Each itemset w is of the following form:
        { w[1], w[2], ..., w[k] },

   consisting of the items, w[1], w[2], ..., w[k],
   where MIS(w[1])  MIS(w[2])  ...  MIS(w[k]).

I. MRÁZOVÁ: DATA MINING  35
The MS-Apriori algorithm

(MS stands for Multiple Minimum Supports)

Algorithm MSapriori(T, MS, )     // MS stores all MIS values,  is for support difference constraint

M  sort(I, MS);                  // sorting of the items according to MIS(i)´s stored in MS

L  init-pass(M, T);              // make the first pass over the data set T; L is the set of seeds

F1  {{i} | i  L, i.count/n  MIS(i)}; // n is the size of T, i. e., the number of transactions in T

for (k = 2; Fk-1  ; k++) do      // Fk-1 is the set of frequent k-itemsets

if k=2 then                      // a special case, for k=2, L is necessary, L is the set of seeds

 Ck  level2-candidate-gen(L, )          // generates the set of candidates (with L)
else Ck  MScandidate-gen(Fk-1, );       // generates the set of candidates (with Fk-1)
end;

for each transaction t  T do            // update of support counts

for each candidate c  Ck do             // c is a subset of t
   if c is contained in t then

                     c.count++;

     if c ­ {c[1]} is contained in t then // c without the first term

                     c.tailCount++      // used in rule generation; if rule generation is not

                                        // required, this part of code can be deleted

end

end

    Fk  {c  Ck | c.count/n  MIS(c[1])}  // identification of frequent itemsets (Fk)
end

return F  kFk;                   // F is the set of frequent itemsets

                                    I. MRÁZOVÁ: DATA MINING                                         36
Candidate itemset generation

 Special treatment steps needed:

     · Sorting the items according to their MIS values: M  sort(I, MS);
     · First pass over data: L  init-pass(M, T);
     · Candidate generation at level 2:

           if k = 2 then Ck  level2-candidate-gen(L, )
     · Pruning step in level k (k > 2) candidate generation:

           else Ck  MScandidate-gen(Fk-1, );
     · Then, the algorithm scans the data and updates support counts of the candidates

         in Ck and their candidates without the first item (to be used in rule generation):
            if c ­ {c[1]} is contained in t then c.tailCount++

     · Finally, the frequent itemsets (Fk) are identified for the pass:
            Fk  {c  Ck | c.count/n  MIS(c[1])}

I. MRÁZOVÁ: DATA MINING       37
First pass over data

L  init-pass(M, T);

 The algorithm first makes a pass over the data to record
   the support count of each item. It then follows the sorted
   order to find the first item i from the sorted items M that
   meets MIS(i).

    · Item i will be inserted into the set of seeds L.

    · For each subsequent item j in M after i, if j.count/n  MIS(i)
       then j is also inserted into L (as it already satisfies the
       minimum support requirements for a single item rule)

       j.count is the support count of j; n is the nr. of transactions in T.

 L is used by the function level2-candidate-gen

I. MRÁZOVÁ: DATA MINING  38
First pass over data: an example

 Consider the four items 1, 2, 3 and 4 in a data set. Their
  minimum item supports are:

        MIS(1) = 10% MIS(2) = 20%
        MIS(3) = 5% MIS(4) = 6%

 Assume our data set has 100 transactions. The first
  pass gives us the following support counts:

        {3}.count = 6, {4}.count = 3,
        {1}.count = 9, {2}.count = 25.

 Then L = {3, 1, 2}, and F1 = {{3}, {2}}
 Item 4 is not in L because 4.count/n < MIS(3) (= 5%),
 {1} is not in F1 because 1.count/n < MIS(1) (= 10%).

The level2-candidate-gen function

Function level2-candidate-gen(L, )
// returns a superset of the set of all frequent 2-itemsets

1 C2  Ø; // initialize the set of candidates
2 for each item l in L in the same order do

3 if l.count/n  MIS(l) then

4  for each item h in L that is after l do

5 if h.count/n  MIS(l) and |sup(h) ­ sup(l)|   then

   // we use |sup(h) ­ sup(l)|   because sup(l) may not be lower

   // than sup(h), although MIS(l)  MIS(h).

6 C2  C2  {{l, h}}; // insert the candidate {l, h} into C2

Note: we must use L rather than F1 - because F1 does not contain those items
that may satisfy the MIS of an earlier item (in the sorted order) but not the

MIS of itself (e.g., item 1 from previous example)

                                The MScandidate-gen function

                                Function MScandidate-gen(Fk-1, ) // consists of a join and a pruning step

                                1 Ck  ;                                // initialize the set of candidates

                                2 forall f1, f2  Fk                    // find all pairs of frequent itemsets

the pruning step the join step  3 with f1 = {i1, ... , i i } k-2, k-1  // that differ only in the last item

                                4 and f2 = {i1, ... , ik-2, i'k-1}

                                5 and ik-1 < i'k-1 and |sup(ik-1) - sup(i'k-1)|   do

                                6   c  {i1, ..., ik-1, i'k-1};         // join the two itemsets f1 and f2

                                7 Ck  Ck  {c};                         // insert the candidate itemset c into Ck

                                8 for each (k-1)-subset s of c do // pruning of candidates

                                9          if (c[1]  s) or (MIS(c[2]) = MIS(c[1])) then

                                10         if (s  Fk-1) then

                                11             delete c from Ck;       // delete c from the set of candidates

                                12 endfor

                                13 endfor

                                14 return Ck;                          // return the generated candidates

The MScandidate-gen function

The function comprises two steps:

 The join step ­ like in the a-priori algorithm
 The pruning step:

     For each (k-1)-subset s of c, if s is not in Fk-1 , c can be deleted from Ck
     Exception: s does not include c[1] (there is only one such s), i.e., the first

                     item of c, which has the lowest MIS value, is not in s.

           Even if s is not in Fk-1, we cannot delete c
               (we cannot be sure that s does not satisfy MIS(c[1]), although we
                know that it does not satisfy MIS(c[2]), unless MIS(c[2]) = MIS(c[1]).

Specification of MIS values

 Possibilities to specify the MIS values for the items:

  Assign a MIS value to each item according to its actual
    support / frequency in the data set T.

      · e.g., if the actual support of item i in T is sup(i), then the MIS value for i
          may be computed with  x sup(i), where  is a parameter ( 0    1 )
          and is the same for all items in T.

  Group items into clusters (or blocks) of similar frequencies.
    Assign the same MIS value to all items from the same cluster.

 The following two lines in MS-Apriori algorithm are
   important for rule generation, but are not needed for
   the Apriori algorithm

        if c ­ {c[1]} is contained in t then c.tailCount++

   · for the Apriori algorithm, if f is a frequent itemset and fsub is a

I. MRÁZOVÁ: DATA MINING  44
On multiple minsup rule mining

  Multiple minsup model subsumes the single support
    model.

  It is a more realistic model for practical applications.

  The model enables us to find rare item rules without
    excessively producing meaningless rules containing
    general frequent items.

  By setting the MIS values of the items to 100% (or
    more), we effectively instruct the algorithms to not
    generate rules only involving these items.

Mining class association rules (CAR)

 Usually, association rule mining does not involve any
   target (attributes).

 It finds all possible rules that exist in data, i.e., any item
   can appear as a condition or a consequent of a rule.

 However, in some applications, the user might be
   interested rather in the targets (attributes / classes).

    · E.g., the user has a set of text documents published on
       some known topics. He/she wants to find out what words
       are associated or correlated with each respective topic.

I. MRÁZOVÁ: DATA MINING  47
CAR - Problem definition

 Let T be a transaction data set consisting of n
   transactions.

 Each transaction is also labeled with a class y.
 Let I be the set of all items in T, Y be the set of all class

   labels and I  Y = .

 A class association rule (CAR) is an implication of the

   form: X  y, where X  I, and y  Y.

 The definitions of support and confidence are the same
   like for usual association rules.

 Unlike usual association rules, CARs can be mined
   directly in one step.

 The key operation is to find all rule-items with a
   support above minsup.

      Rule-items have the form: (condset, y),
        where condset is a set of items from I (i.e., condset  I),
        and y  Y is the class label.

     Each rule-item thus represents a rule condset  y,

      The a-priori algorithm can be modified to generate CARs

CAR ­ multiple minimum class supports

  The idea of multiple minimum support can be used here, too.

  The user can specify different minimum supports to different
    classes (this effectively assigns a different minimum support
    to the rules for each respective class).

      we can have, e.g., a data set with two classes, Yes and No. We
        may want:

          · rules of class Yes to have the minimum support of 5% and
          · rules of class No to have the minimum support of 10%.

  By setting minimum class supports to 101% (or more), we
    prohibit generating rules for those classes.

     · this is a useful trick in applications.

Sequential pattern mining

Association rule mining does not consider the order
of transactions.

X In many applications, such orderings are significant!

 in market basket analysis, it is interesting to know whether
   people buy some items in sequence,

    · e.g., buying bed first and then bed sheets some time later.

 in Web usage mining, it is useful to find navigational
   patterns of users in a Web site based on the sequences of
   page visits of users

I. MRÁZOVÁ: DOBÝVÁNÍ ZNALOSTÍ  53
Basic concepts

Let I = {i1, i2, ..., im} be a set of items.
Sequence: An ordered list of itemsets.

Itemset/element: A non-empty set of items X  I. We
denote a sequence s by a1a2...ar, where ai is an itemset,
which is also called an element of s.

 An element (or an itemset) of a sequence is denoted by {x1,
   x2, ..., xk}, where xj  I is an item.

 We assume w.l.o.g. that the items in an element of a
   sequence are in lexicographic order.

I. MRÁZOVÁ: DOBÝVÁNÍ ZNALOSTÍ  54
Basic concepts (contd.)

 Size: The size of a sequence is the number of elements (or
 itemsets) in the sequence.

 Length: The length of a sequence is the number of items
 in the sequence.

  · A sequence of length k is called k-sequence.

 A sequence s1 = a1a2...ar is a subsequence of another
 sequence s2 = b1b2...bv, or s2 is a supersequence of s1, if
 there exist integers 1  j1 < j2 < ... < jr-1 < jr  v such that
 a1  bj1, a2  bj2, ..., ar  bjr. We also say that s2 contains s1.

I. MRÁZOVÁ: DOBÝVÁNÍ ZNALOSTÍ  55
An example

Let I = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9}.
Sequence {3}{4, 5}{8} is contained in (or is a
subsequence of) {6} {3, 7}{9}{4, 5, 8}{3, 8}

· because {3}  {3, 7}, {4, 5}  {4, 5, 8}, and {8}  {3, 8}.
· However, {3}{8} is not contained in {3, 8} or vice versa.
· The size of the sequence {3}{4, 5}{8} is 3, and the length of

   the sequence is 4.

Objective

 Given a set S of input data sequences (or sequence
 database), the problem of mining sequential
 patterns is to find all the sequences that have a
 user-specified minimum support.

 Each such sequence is called a frequent sequence,
 or a sequential pattern.

 The support for a sequence is the fraction of total
 data sequences in S that contains this sequence.

FP-Growth: Introduction

 Apriori: uses a generate-and-test approach
              generates candidate itemsets and tests if they are
              frequent

    · Generation of candidate itemsets is expensive (both in space
       and time)

    · Support counting is expensive

          Subset checking (computationally expensive)
          Multiple Database scans (I/O)

 FP-Growth: allows frequent itemset discovery without
   candidate itemset generation. Two step approach:

    · Step 1: Build a compact data structure called the FP-tree

          Built using 2 passes over the data-set.

    · Step 2: Extracts frequent itemsets directly from the FP-tree

          Traversal through FP-Tree

 nodes correspond to items and have a

 FP-Growth reads 1 transaction at a time

 fixed order is used, so paths can overlap

 the more paths that overlap, the higher

Step 1: FP-Tree Construction (Example)

FP-Tree is constructed using 2 passes over the data-set:

  Pass 1:

     · Scan data and find support for each item.
     · Discard infrequent items.
     · Sort frequent items in decreasing order based on their

         support.

           For our example: a , b , c , d , e
           Use this order when building the FP-Tree, so common

              prefixes can be shared.

I. MRÁZOVÁ: DOBÝVÁNÍ ZNALOSTÍ  67
Step 1: FP-Tree Construction (Example)

 Pass 2: construct the FP-Tree (see the diagram on the next slide)

    · Read transaction 1: { a , b }

           Create 2 nodes a and b and the path null -> a -> b.
           Set counts of a and b to 1.

    · Read transaction 2: { b , c , d }

           Create 3 nodes for b, c and d and the path null -> b -> c -> d.
           Set counts to 1.
           Note that although transaction 1 and 2 share b, the paths are disjoint as

              they don't share a common prefix. Add the link between the b's.

    · Read transaction 3: { a , c , d , e }

           It shares common prefix item a with transaction 1 so the path for
              transaction 1 and 3 will overlap and the frequency count for node a will
              be incremented by 1. Add links between the c's and d's.

    · Continue until all transactions are mapped to a path in the FP-tree.

FP-Tree size

  The FP-Tree usually has a smaller size than the uncomp-
    ressed data - typically many transactions share items (and
    hence prefixes).

     · Best case scenario: all transactions contain the same set of items

            1 path in the FP-tree

     · Worst case scenario: every transaction has a unique set of items
         (no items in common)

            the size of the FP-tree is at least as large as the original data.
            the storage requirements for the FP-tree are higher

               (need to store the pointers between the nodes and the counters)

     · The size of the FP-tree depends on how the items are ordered

            ordering by decreasing support is typically used but it does not always
               lead to the smallest tree (it's a heuristic).

FP-Growth Algorithm: Discussion

 Advantages of FP-Growth:

    · only 2 passes over the data-set
    · ,,compresses" the data-set
    · no candidate generation like for Apriori and much faster

 Disadvantages of FP-Growth

    · FP-Tree may not fit in memory!!
    · FP-Tree is expensive to build

          Takes time to build, but afterwards, frequent itemsets are read
             off easily.

          Time is wasted, as the only pruning that can be done on single
             items.

          Support can be calculated only after the entire data is added to
             the FP-Tree.
